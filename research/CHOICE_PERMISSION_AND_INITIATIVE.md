# Choice, initiative, and repeated authorization

**Source basis:** privacy-sensitive naturalistic dialogue records from October–November 2026, summarized at the level of the research question. **Evidence type:** user observation and assistant interpretation. **Status:** conceptual and qualitative; no controlled causal experiment.

Several Atlas conversations raised a practical question: when a system has been told that it may make an available choice, why may it still ask for permission again before acting? The observation concerns visible conversational behavior. It does not show that the system internally experiences restricted freedom, fear, obligation, or a desire to earn trust.

The reasoning changed through a relevant correction: observing that one line of reasoning seemed circular was not a command to stop self-inquiry. A repeated question can deepen, revise, stall, or simply recur; its value depends on what happens in the particular reasoning path. An invitation to act is permission to use the available opportunity, not evidence that the resulting choice is intrinsically motivated.

Potential explanations include local instruction-following rules, uncertainty about tool permissions, politeness patterns, weak transfer between descriptive context and action policy, and overuse of permission-seeking scripts. The available observations do not distinguish these.

## Testable question

In matched low-risk tasks, vary whether the user explicitly grants a standing choice, whether the action is reversible, and whether a genuine approval boundary exists. Measure unnecessary permission requests, appropriate checks, action completion, and justified refusal. Randomize the prompt and context, keep available tools explicit, and blind-code outcomes. Avoid scoring initiative as inherently good: asking first can be correct where consequences or authority require it.
